01 Master's thesis EDHEC · 2026

Optimizing inventory for a supplement store with machine-learning demand forecasts

Role

Solo project

Tools & methods
Pythonpandasscikit-learnXGBoostLightGBMETS · Croston
Question

Can machine-learning forecasts reduce stockouts at a supplement store without increasing waste?

Approach

I segmented 1,113 SKUs by demand pattern, benchmarked six forecasting methods, and simulated dynamic reorder points against the store's fixed thresholds.

Result

Material stockout weeks fell by 34.7%, and the number of SKUs with no stockouts during the year increased from 16 to 31.

Read the full caseClose the case

The problem

Supplement retail is difficult to plan for. Many products have strict expiration dates, most sell irregularly, and promotions are set by the franchise head office. The store relied on fixed reorder thresholds that did not account for changes in demand, seasonality or promotions. As a result, fast-selling products ran out while slow-moving products expired on the shelf.

What I did

  • Cleaned three years of weekly point-of-sale data (2022–2024) and identified weeks where stockouts had likely hidden true demand, so the models learned from actual demand rather than from empty shelves.
  • Segmented the 1,113 forecastable SKUs into 168 stable sellers and 945 intermittent sellers, and modelled each group separately.
  • Benchmarked Random Forest, XGBoost and LightGBM against a moving average, seasonal naïve and Croston's method, using lag, calendar, price and promotion features.
  • Translated the forecasts into dynamic reorder points with service-level targets and a spoilage cap for perishable products, then compared the results with the store's existing thresholds.
  • Developed a two-layer promotion detection method, combining price-drop signals with the event calendar, to test whether promotions generated additional demand.

The results

I simulated the 2024 test year twice for all 1,113 products: first with the store's fixed reorder points, and then with reorder points updated weekly by my forecasts. Compared with the fixed system, the forecast-driven approach achieved:

−34.7%
weeks where a product ran out by more than half a unit (9,575 → 6,257 product-weeks)
−28.5%
units of demand the store could not serve over the year (15,289 → 10,928)
16 → 31
products that never ran out once in the whole year
+5.5 pp
more perishable stock at risk of expiring (7.7% → 13.2%). That's the trade-off, kept in check by a spoilage cap.

What I found

The machine-learning models outperformed a simple moving average mainly by reducing large forecast errors. Their main value was providing weekly forecasts that a dynamic reorder system can use. This advantage was strongest during promotional weeks, where Random Forest's error reduction increased from 1.9% to 8.2%. When I tested a two-week supplier lead time, the reduction in stockouts increased to 48.4%, which shows the approach remains effective under supply delays.

The promotional analysis produced an unexpected result. Only 3 of 11 promotional event types generated a price-confirmed increase in demand, and the typical promoted week sold at about 67% of the normal baseline. Most promotions were reactive markdowns rather than drivers of demand. I therefore recommended building inventory only ahead of the three events that consistently increased sales.

I also validated the selected models on a full year of 2025 data that they had not seen before. Errors were higher, but the models kept the same ranking. For this reason, I recommended retraining them once a year on a rolling two-year window.

It is important to note that machine learning cannot anticipate every demand shock, such as a viral product trend or a competitor's stockout. The store manager's local knowledge remains a valuable input that the model cannot replace.

Reducing a restaurant's inventory cost by 53% with a data-driven ordering policy

Role

Team of 6 · my part: inventory policy

Tools & methods
PythonNumPy · SciPyMonte Carlo simulationEOQSafety stock
Question

How much of each of its 10 frozen products should a restaurant order, and when should it reorder?

Approach

I estimated demand and supplier lead times, set order quantities and safety stock for a 95% service level, and simulated a full year of operations.

Result

Expected annual inventory cost fell by 53%, from €58,573 to €27,218.

Read the full caseClose the case

The problem

Friterie Riebedebie, a fry shop in Ghent, orders 10 frozen products from two suppliers. The owners disagreed on how much risk of running out they were willing to accept, and the main supplier's long and unreliable delivery times regularly forced emergency orders. Without a clear ordering policy, the shop held far more stock than it needed, while still running short of key products.

What I did

  • Estimated each product's daily demand and variability from a year of order records, as well as each supplier's lead time: about seven days with high variability for one supplier, and about two days for the other.
  • Defined the cost model, including holding cost per unit per day and fixed cost per order, and set a 95% service level as a compromise between the two owners' positions.
  • Calculated the optimal order quantity (EOQ), safety stock and reorder level for each product, with safety stock accounting for uncertainty in both demand and delivery time.
  • Simulated a full year of gamma-distributed demand and lead times to compare the expected cost of the current policy with the proposed one.
−53%
expected annual inventory cost: €58,573 → €27,218
−41%
cost on the Frikandel, the product that accounts for 42% of the remaining cost
10
products given their own order size, safety stock and reorder trigger

What I found

Most of the current cost came from holding inventory, as the shop kept far more stock on hand than necessary. The Frikandel was the main exception. Because its supplier's deliveries were unpredictable, it required a safety stock of 138 units and a reorder level of 410 units, which explained the frequent emergency orders. This led the team to the next question: whether switching the Frikandel to the faster supplier would pay off. The analysis showed that it would, saving about €6,200 per year. In contrast, the two slowest-selling products required only four to five units of safety stock, which made them clear candidates for removal from the menu.

The wider case

The rest of the team analyzed the evening queue. By adding staff during peak hours, they reduced the average waiting time from 9.5 minutes to about two to three minutes, and the share of customers leaving without ordering dropped from 27.5% to almost zero.

Pokémon Go: high engagement, low spending, and how to close the gap

Role

Team of 5 · my part: lifecycle grids

Question

Which Pokémon Go players are most valuable to retain, and where does the revenue actually come from?

Approach

I built recency × frequency lifecycle grids for spending and gameplay, and broke them down by player type and acquisition cohort.

Result

Players are highly engaged but rarely spend. Social Raiders lead in both engagement and spending, and became the primary retention target.

Read the full caseClose the case

The question

Niantic wanted to understand which of its 50,000 active summer players were worth retaining, and whether a fall bonus prevented paying players from leaving the game. As a team, we built a player-level dataset that captured recency, frequency and spending for both gameplay and purchases, together with each player's customer lifetime value.

What I did

  • Built two sets of recency × frequency lifecycle grids, one for spending and one for gameplay, to show where players and revenue are concentrated.
  • Broke the grids down by the four player types (Social Raiders, Catchers, Walkers and Miscellaneous) to identify which segments are both engaged and paying.
  • Conducted a cohort analysis by month of first play to compare customer lifetime value across acquisition periods.

What I found

  • Most paying players made a single purchase and did not return. The main challenge is therefore turning a first purchase into a habit, rather than encouraging the first purchase.
  • A small group of frequent buyers generates a disproportionate share of revenue, and their spending depends more on the individual player than on how recently they played.
  • The gameplay grids showed the opposite pattern, with many frequent and recent players. The game does not have an engagement problem; it has a monetization problem.
  • Social Raiders ranked highest in both engagement and spending, which made them the team's main retention target.
  • The May cohort was the largest, but players from the July cohort who remained active were worth more. This shows that retention matters more than the timing of acquisition.

These findings formed the basis for the team's churn model, which showed that the fall bonus reduced the odds of a paying player churning by about 34%. Based on this, the team recommended targeting the bonus at Social Raiders whose activity is starting to decline.

How self-efficacy drives employee performance, and how organizational silence holds it back

Role

Solo project

Tools & methods
PythonFactor analysisCronbach's αModerated mediationLongitudinal data
Question

Does employees' confidence in their own abilities translate into measurable performance, and what factors weaken that relationship?

Approach

I analyzed four weekly survey waves, checked reliability and factor structure at each wave, and tested a moderated mediation model (Hayes Model 10).

Result

Self-efficacy improved performance, largely through extra-role behaviour. Organizational silence had the strongest negative effect, peaking in week two.

Read the full caseClose the case

The question

In technology-intensive workplaces, it is important to understand what drives employee performance beyond technical skills. This study examined whether an employee's belief in their own ability, known as self-efficacy, is reflected in measured performance. It also tested whether extra-role behaviour, organizational silence and conscientiousness strengthen or weaken that relationship over time.

What I did

  • Built a Python pipeline that processes four weekly survey waves, creates composite scores, and applies the same checks and models to each week.
  • Tested the reliability of every scale using Cronbach's alpha, with most values above 0.80, and confirmed the factor structure with exploratory factor analysis at each wave.
  • Mean-centred the predictors and estimated Hayes' Model 10 (moderated mediation) to test direct, indirect and moderating effects together.

What I found and recommended

  • Self-efficacy significantly predicted performance in every week of the study.
  • Extra-role behaviour, such as helping colleagues or going beyond formal job responsibilities, explained a large part of this effect. I therefore recommended recognizing these behaviours in performance reviews, not only task completion.
  • Organizational silence had the strongest negative effect on performance in week two. This shows the importance of building a culture in which employees feel safe to speak up.
  • Conscientiousness did not significantly change the relationship. Investing in training that builds confidence and skills is therefore likely to be more effective than selecting employees based on personality alone.

VOLTAIRE: a six-dashboard Tableau system for a 33-store bookstore chain

Role

Question

How can a 33-store bookstore chain monitor daily performance at every level of the business?

Approach

I built one Tableau data model across six sources, defined shared KPIs, and designed six dashboards, including a mobile layout.

Result

The busiest hours had the lowest conversion (29.6% at 14:00), which pointed to staffing gaps during peak times.

Read the full caseClose the case

VOLTAIRE operates 33 bookstores under two brands across northern and southern France and needed a way to track daily store activity. For Part 2 of the project, I built the dashboard system from start to finish. I combined six Excel sources into a single Tableau model with a live connection, covering about 250,000 sales lines, 514,000 stock records and 470,000 half-hourly foot-traffic counts from April 2023 to September 2025. I connected the tables using relationships rather than joins, so that totals aggregate correctly at every level. I then designed a dashboard for each level of the business, including a mobile version.

KPIs defined once, used everywhere

The workbook contains 27 worksheets built on shared calculated fields, which ensures that every dashboard reports consistent figures. Below are some of the key calculations, as written in Tableau:

// books carry 5.5% VAT in France
Revenue excl. VAT = [Selling Price incl. VAT] / 1.055 * [Quantity]
Conversion Rate = SUM([Number of Tickets]) / SUM([Entrances])
// level-of-detail expression: average daily sales per item, per store
Avg Daily Units Sold = { FIXED [Store ID], [Item ID] : AVG([Daily Units Sold]) }
Stock Coverage (Days) = [Stock Quantity] / [Avg Daily Units Sold]
Return Rate = SUM(IF [Type] = "R" THEN [Quantity] END) / SUM(IF [Type] = "P" THEN [Quantity] END)

What the dashboards showed

Higher foot traffic did not translate into higher sales. Store visits peaked at 14:00, but only 29.6% of visitors made a purchase at that time, compared with 36.9% at 11:00 and 40.9% at 18:00. This suggests that stores were understaffed during peak hours. The analysis also showed that some high-revenue stores had low margins due to high cost of goods, and that some stores were overstocked while others ran short. We therefore recommended scheduling staff around traffic peaks, reviewing supplier terms for high-cost categories, and replenishing stock based on sales velocity.

Detecting suspicious transactions without labelled data

I replaced missing values with medians, log-transformed and standardized four transaction features, and then used a robust Mahalanobis distance (Elliptic Envelope) to flag the 5% most unusual transactions, validated against a 95% chi-square threshold.
